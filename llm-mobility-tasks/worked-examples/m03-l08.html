Extracting facts into a structured table with page citations — worked example

After working through this example you will be able to

  • Write a scoped extraction prompt that names exact indicators, restricts an assistant to a single uploaded document, and specifies a table format with a page-citation column.
  • Extract a five-row indicator table (indicator, value, page) from a long municipal monitoring report and trace each figure back to its source sentence.
  • Verify cited page numbers against a PDF viewer's own page counter and the value actually printed there, and mark rows confirmed or unconfirmed accordingly.
  • Recognise when a document's tables might be scanned images rather than digital text, and check for that risk before trusting any extracted row.

You work in Gdańsk's mobility unit. Your manager wants a short indicator table to attach to a note comparing 2022 outcomes against target, and she has been explicit: every number in that table has to point to a page, because if the Programme Coordination team challenges her on it, she needs to open the PDF and show them. You've been handed the city's 2022 monitoring report for the Mobility and Transport Operational Programme — a digital-text PDF, not scanned — and asked to pull five specific figures out of it into a table she can paste straight into her note.

The five figures she needs are: total length of cycling routes at end of 2022, total public transport passengers carried in 2022, percentage of public roads with a speed limit of 30 km/h or lower in 2022, number of registered electric and hybrid vehicles in 2022, and the average passenger rating of tram communication in 2022. The table can use only this one report as its source — no other Gdańsk plan, no prior knowledge — and must have exactly three columns: indicator, value, page.

Step 0 — Checking the brief's own page-count claim before starting

The materials that came with this task describe the source as a '120-odd-page monitoring report.' Having actually fetched and paginated the file, it runs to 32 pages: page 1 blank cover, page 2 title page, pages 3–31 content, page 32 closing credits and publisher block. That's worth flagging up front, both because it's wrong and because it matters later — vendor page-count thresholds for how PDFs get processed change the risk picture, and this document sits well under the threshold that would matter here.[1]

Step 1 — The vague prompt someone might try first

Summarise the KPIs in this report.

This is not safe to hand to a manager because it lets the assistant choose which of the dozens of figures in the report's 'Fakty i liczby' section and its four 'Wskaźniki realizacji' tables count as KPIs, in what wording, with no obligation to say where each number sits in the PDF — a fluent paragraph could blend a 2022 figure with a 2016–2022 trend line, or paraphrase a value instead of quoting it, and there would be no page anchor for anyone to check.

Step 2 — The scoped extraction prompt

You have been given one PDF: the City of Gdańsk report "Raport z realizacji Programu Operacyjnego VIII. Mobilność i Transport za 2022 rok." Use only this uploaded document — do not use any outside knowledge, other Gdańsk plans, or prior conversation context.

Extract exactly these five 2022 figures:
1. Total length of cycling routes in Gdańsk at the end of 2022
2. Total number of public transport passengers carried in 2022
3. Percentage of public roads in Gdańsk with a speed limit of 30 km/h or lower in 2022
4. Number of registered electric and hybrid vehicles in Gdańsk in 2022
5. Average passenger rating of tram communication in 2022 (state the rating scale)

For each, quote the exact sentence or table row the number comes from, and give the PDF page it appears on. If a figure does not appear verbatim in the document, say so instead of estimating. Output as a table with exactly three columns: indicator | value | page. State whether "page" refers to the number printed on the page or your PDF viewer's own page count.

Thinking out loud

The prompt asked for 'newly registered' EVs and hybrids in 2022, but the sentence on page 7 gives a stock count — vehicles registered in Gdańsk as of 2022, not new registrations during the year. I checked the four indicator tables at the back of the report and found no separate 'new registrations in year' indicator for vehicles anywhere in the document. Labelling this row as new registrations would put a claim in front of a funder that the source document doesn't actually support, so the honest move is to report it as the registered-stock figure and say so plainly, rather than force the label to match the original request.

That mismatch matters enough to flag on its own: the table above reports row 4 as the count of registered electric and hybrid vehicles as of 2022, not new registrations during 2022, because the report does not contain a new-registrations-per-year figure for vehicles.

Step 4 — Verifying two rows against the cited pages

Checking a cited page means landing on that exact page using the viewer's own page counter and confirming the value is actually printed there — not just trusting the citation. For the cycling-routes row, the page tagged 7 by the viewer also carried the printed footer number 7, and its text contained the 'Fakty i liczby' heading followed directly by the sentence with 868.5 km. Page counter, printed footer, and the target value all agreed: confirmed.[1]

For the tram-rating row, the page tagged 20 by the viewer also carried the printed footer number 20, and its text contained the sentence about the 3.40 tram rating, appearing right after the equivalent bus rating of 3.08 in the same passage describing the 2022 ZTM passenger survey. Page counter, printed footer, and the target value all agreed: confirmed.[1]

The page-numbering convention used for both checks was the number printed on the page (the footer), which in this document equals the PDF viewer's own running page count from page 3 onward — there's no offset trap here because the unnumbered cover and title page precede a footer count that starts cleanly at 3 on the third physical page.[1]

Step 5 — The scanned-image risk, and how to check for it

If this report's indicator tables turned out to be scanned images rather than digital text, the risk depends on which assistant and how long the document is. Anthropic's help page states that Claude analyzes both text and visual elements in PDFs of 100 pages or fewer, but for PDFs from 101 to 1000 pages processes text only and does not analyze visual elements.[2]

Claude analyzes both text and visual elements (like images, charts, and graphics) in PDFs of 100 pages or fewer. For PDFs from 101 to 1000 pages, Claude processes text only and doesn't analyze visual elements.[2]

This report is 32 pages, well under that 100-page line, so a scanned table in it would still be visually read by Claude and a number could still come back — but that number would be a vision-model reading of pixels rather than a text-layer extraction, so an OCR misread (say, a smudged '148,0' read as '146,0') is a real risk with no underlying text layer to cross-check it against. If the same file crossed 100 pages, Claude would drop into text-only mode and a scanned table would simply be invisible to it: the indicator might be silently omitted, or worse, a plausible-looking value could appear with a page citation that doesn't actually contain that text.[2]

OpenAI's help center confirms a related but distinct risk for ChatGPT: for non-PDF document types, text is extracted digitally and any images are discarded outright.[3]

ChatGPT will extract digital text from the file and discard any images[3]

So if anyone re-saves the report as a Word document or similar before uploading, the same silent-discard risk applies with a vendor-specific twist. The practical check, regardless of vendor: open the PDF and try to select or search the text of the specific table cell — highlight it with the cursor, or use Find to search for one of the numbers. If the text highlights or the search finds it, there's a real text layer behind that page and it's safe to cite. If nothing highlights and a search for the number returns zero hits, that page is an image, and no page citation drawn from an assistant's reading of it should go in front of the Programme Coordination team without a manual, eyes-on read of the actual scanned page first.

Takeaways

  • A vague summarise request lets the assistant choose which figures matter and in what wording, with no page anchor — unsafe to hand to a manager or funder.
  • A scoped extraction prompt that names the exact indicators, restricts the assistant to one uploaded document, and demands a three-column table (indicator, value, page) produces a traceable result.
  • The page-citation column is a claim, not a guarantee — each cited page must be opened using the viewer's own page counter and checked against the printed number and the actual value before the row is trusted.
  • If a requested figure doesn't match what the source document actually contains (e.g. a stock count instead of a new-registrations count), report what the document actually says rather than relabeling it to fit the request.
  • Whether a scanned table is silently missed or silently misread depends on document length and vendor — check by trying to select or search the text of a table cell before trusting any row extracted from it.
