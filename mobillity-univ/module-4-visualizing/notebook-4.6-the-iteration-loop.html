The Iteration Loop — building charts by talking to an LLM¶

The question. On a single day, are trips spread evenly across Spain's provinces, or concentrated in a few big ones? By trip we mean one person moving from an origin district to a destination district that day — everyday mobility across all modes of transport, not a single kind of vehicle.

Why it's worth asking. Whether mobility is even or concentrated tells you where demand — and the case for investment — actually sits. It's the kind of question a single well-chosen chart can answer at a glance.

The data. One synthetic day of province-to-province flows: a small, shareable stand-in for the real national file (same columns, illustrative volumes). Each row is the estimated number of trips from one origin district to one destination district; the first two digits of the origin code are its province. Those counts are population-reweighted estimates, so they arrive as decimals rather than whole people.

This notebook is a worked example of the iteration loop. We never hand-write the final chart. We describe what we want, the model writes the code, we run it, look at what came back, and describe the next change — generate → look → name the gap → ask again — until both charts answer the question.

1. Start from the question — ask which chart fits¶

Before any plotting code, describe the data and the question to the model and ask which chart types would show concentration. A possible prompt is as follows:

I have a mobility dataset and want to analyze whether trips on a given day are spread evenly across Spain’s provinces or concentrated in a few large provinces.

The dataset is available here:
https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pmarcelino/mobillity-courses/main/mobillity-univ/module-4-visualizing/data/main/mobillity-univ/module-4-visualizing/data/mitma-spain-od-synthetic/province_flows_practice.csv

Suggest suitable chart types for this analysis.

For each chart type, briefly explain:

1. What question it helps answer.
2. What variables from the dataset it would likely use.
3. Why it is useful for identifying whether trips are evenly distributed or concentrated.

And a possible answer is as follows:

I'll take a look at the actual dataset first so my chart suggestions match its real structure. Good — I can see the structure. Four columns: `fecha` (date, all `20250303` here, i.e. a single day), `origen` and `destino` (5-digit location codes), and `viajes` (trip counts, e.g. `725.035`).

One thing that shapes every chart below: the province isn't a column on its own. In Spain's INE coding, the province is the **first two digits** of the `origen`/`destino` codes (01 = Álava, 08 = Barcelona, 28 = Madrid, …, 52 = Melilla). So the prep step is to extract that 2-digit prefix and sum `viajes` per province. You also need to decide *which* province you mean — origin province, destination province, or both — since a trip has two endpoints. For "are trips concentrated," aggregating by origin and by destination separately (then comparing) is usually the most informative.

You can already smell concentration in the raw rows: Madrid (28…) and Barcelona (08…) flows sit in the thousands while most others are in the hundreds.

Here are the chart types I'd suggest, strongest first.

**1. Ranked bar chart (ideally a Pareto chart)**
- *Question:* Which provinces carry the most trips, and how steep is the fall-off from the top ones?
- *Variables:* province (2-digit prefix of `origen` or `destino`) on the x-axis, summed `viajes` on the y-axis, bars sorted descending. Add a cumulative-percentage line on a secondary axis to make it a proper Pareto chart.
- *Why it fits:* the *shape* answers the question directly. A steep drop then a long flat tail = concentration; a gently sloping, near-flat profile = even distribution. The cumulative line quantifies it ("the top 3 provinces account for X% of all trips").

**2. Lorenz curve (with a Gini coefficient)**
- *Question:* How unequal is the trip distribution across provinces, as a single comparable number?
- *Variables:* cumulative share of provinces (x) vs. cumulative share of `viajes` (y), after sorting provinces from fewest to most trips.
- *Why it fits:* this is the purpose-built tool for concentration. Perfect evenness lies on the 45° diagonal; the more the curve bows below it, the more concentrated the trips. The Gini coefficient (area between curve and diagonal) collapses that into one 0–1 number, which is ideal if you later want to compare days, or origins vs. destinations.

**3. Choropleth map**
- *Question:* *Where* is the concentration — is it spatial (a few big urban provinces) or scattered?
- *Variables:* province polygons (joined on the 2-digit code) shaded by summed `viajes`.
- *Why it fits:* the ranked chart tells you concentration exists; the map tells you it's Madrid/Barcelona/Valencia rather than a random set. For a mobility dataset the geographic pattern is often the actual insight, and dark clusters vs. a pale background make even-vs-concentrated visually obvious.

**4. Treemap**
- *Question:* What fraction of the whole does each province represent?
- *Variables:* one rectangle per province, area proportional to summed `viajes`.
- *Why it fits:* it's an intuitive part-to-whole view. A few dominant rectangles swallowing the canvas reads instantly as concentration; many similarly-sized tiles reads as even. Good for a non-technical audience where a Lorenz curve wouldn't land.

**5. Histogram of per-province trip totals** (secondary/supporting)
- *Question:* What does the distribution of province-level totals look like?
- *Variables:* summed `viajes` per province, binned into a frequency histogram.
- *Why it fits:* a strong right-skew (many low-trip provinces, a few extreme high-trip ones) is the signature of concentration; a roughly symmetric, tight distribution suggests evenness. Caveat: with only ~52 provinces the bins are coarse, so treat this as corroboration for the Lorenz/Pareto view rather than the headline chart.

If I were choosing two, I'd pair the **Pareto chart** (immediate, readable) with the **Lorenz curve + Gini** (rigorous and comparable), and add the **choropleth** whenever the geography matters.

Want me to actually run this on the file — aggregate by province and generate, say, the Pareto chart and Lorenz curve with a computed Gini — so you can see how concentrated this particular day is?

Let's assume that for this case we will use the ranked bar chart and the choropleth map.

"""
Choropleth map: trips by Spanish province for a single day.

Goal: see whether trips are spread relatively evenly across Spain's
provinces, or concentrated in just a few of them. On a map,
concentration shows up as a couple of dark provinces surrounded by
many pale ones.

We need two ingredients:
  1. The mobility data (how many trips per province)   -> pandas
  2. The shape of each province (the geometry)          -> geopandas
Then we JOIN them on the province code and colour each province by
its number of trips.
"""

import pandas as pd
import geopandas as gpd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 0. Settings you can change
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
CSV_URL = (
    "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pmarcelino/mobillity-courses/"
    "main/mobillity-univ/module-4-visualizing/data/"
    "main/mobillity-univ/module-4-visualizing/data/mitma-spain-od-synthetic/province_flows_practice.csv"
)

# Province boundaries of Spain as GeoJSON. This file has a "cod_prov"
# column = the 2-digit INE province code, which is exactly what we
# need to join on.
GEOJSON_URL = (
    "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/codeforgermany/click_that_hood/"
    "main/public/data/spain-provinces.geojson"
)

SELECTED_DAY = 20250303     # day to analyse (YYYYMMDD)
PROVINCE_FROM = "origen"    # aggregate by trip ORIGIN. Use "destino" for destinations.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Load the mobility data
#    Read the code columns as text so "01187" keeps its leading zero.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
df = pd.read_csv(CSV_URL, dtype={"origen": str, "destino": str})

# The province is the FIRST TWO digits of the 5-digit location code.
df[PROVINCE_FROM] = df[PROVINCE_FROM].str.zfill(5)
df["cod_prov"] = df[PROVINCE_FROM].str[:2]

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Aggregate trips by province for the selected day
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
day_df = df[df["fecha"] == SELECTED_DAY]

trips = (
    day_df.groupby("cod_prov", as_index=False)["viajes"]
    .sum()
    .rename(columns={"viajes": "total_trips"})
)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Load the province map of Spain
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
provinces = gpd.read_file(GEOJSON_URL)   # columns include: cod_prov, name, geometry

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Join trip totals onto the province geometries
#    how="left" keeps ALL 52 provinces, even any with zero trips.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
provinces = provinces.merge(trips, on="cod_prov", how="left")
provinces["total_trips"] = provinces["total_trips"].fillna(0)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 5. Draw the choropleth map
#    A sequential colour map (light -> dark) means:
#    more trips = darker province.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(11, 10))

provinces.plot(
    column="total_trips",     # value that decides the colour
    cmap="OrRd",              # light yellow (few trips) -> dark red (many trips)
    linewidth=0.4,
    edgecolor="grey",         # thin borders so provinces are distinguishable
    legend=True,
    legend_kwds={"label": "Total trips", "shrink": 0.6},
    ax=ax,
)

ax.set_title(
    f"Trips by province — {SELECTED_DAY} (by trip {PROVINCE_FROM})\n"
    "Darker = more trips. A few dark provinces = concentration.",
    fontsize=13,
)
ax.axis("off")   # hide the latitude/longitude frame; a map doesn't need it

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig("province_choropleth.png", dpi=150, bbox_inches="tight")
print("Map saved as province_choropleth.png")

# Optional: quick numeric check of concentration
top2 = trips.sort_values("total_trips", ascending=False).head(2)

print(f"Top 2 provinces = {share:.1f}% of all trips")

Quick explanation first, then the Plotly version.

**Why the error happened.** `ModuleNotFoundError: No module named 'geopandas'` means Python looked for a library called `geopandas` and couldn't find it. Python only knows two kinds of modules: the ones built into it, and the ones you've explicitly installed. `geopandas` is neither built-in nor installed by default, so importing it fails until you install it (`pip install geopandas`). The extra wrinkle with geopandas specifically is that it depends on several heavyweight geographic C libraries (GEOS, GDAL, PROJ) that it pulls in behind the scenes — which is a common source of install friction for beginners. So the error isn't about your code being wrong; the toolbox just wasn't on the shelf.

**Why I reached for geopandas originally.** It's the standard, textbook tool for geospatial data in Python: it extends pandas with a `geometry` column, reads GeoJSON straight off disk, and turns a joined table into a choropleth in a single `.plot()` call. That makes it the "default" answer. But it has two costs that matter for you: the heavy install you just ran into, and static (non-interactive) output.

Given your course was built on Plotly, `plotly.express` is genuinely the better fit here. It installs as a single lightweight wheel (no native geo libraries), produces an interactive map where you can hover a province to see its name and trip count, and `px.choropleth` performs the geometry-to-data join internally — so you don't need geopandas at all. """
Choropleth map with PLOTLY: trips by Spanish province for a single day.

Goal: see whether trips are spread relatively evenly across Spain's
provinces, or concentrated in just a few of them. On the map,
concentration shows up as a couple of dark provinces among many pale ones.

Why Plotly (and not geopandas)?
  - Installs as a single lightweight package: `pip install plotly`
    (no heavy geographic C libraries to fight with).
  - The map is INTERACTIVE: hover over a province to see its name and
    trip count; zoom and pan freely.
  - px.choropleth does the geometry <-> data join for us internally,
    so we don't need geopandas at all.
"""

import json
import urllib.request

import pandas as pd
import plotly.express as px

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 0. Settings you can change
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
CSV_URL = (
    "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pmarcelino/mobillity-courses/"
    "main/mobillity-univ/module-4-visualizing/data/"
    "main/mobillity-univ/module-4-visualizing/data/mitma-spain-od-synthetic/province_flows_practice.csv"
)

# Province boundaries of Spain as GeoJSON. Each feature has a
# properties.cod_prov field = the 2-digit INE province code we join on.
GEOJSON_URL = (
    "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/codeforgermany/click_that_hood/"
    "main/public/data/spain-provinces.geojson"
)

SELECTED_DAY = 20250303     # day to analyse (YYYYMMDD)
PROVINCE_FROM = "origen"    # aggregate by trip ORIGIN. Use "destino" for destinations.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Load the mobility data
#    Read the code columns as text so "01187" keeps its leading zero.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
df = pd.read_csv(CSV_URL, dtype={"origen": str, "destino": str})

# The province is the FIRST TWO digits of the 5-digit location code.
df[PROVINCE_FROM] = df[PROVINCE_FROM].str.zfill(5)
df["cod_prov"] = df[PROVINCE_FROM].str[:2]

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Aggregate trips by province for the selected day
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
day_df = df[df["fecha"] == SELECTED_DAY]

trips = (
    day_df.groupby("cod_prov", as_index=False)["viajes"]
    .sum()
    .rename(columns={"viajes": "total_trips"})
)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Load the province map of Spain (GeoJSON as a Python dict)
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
with urllib.request.urlopen(GEOJSON_URL) as response:
    spain_geojson = json.load(response)

# Add a readable province name to each row (from the GeoJSON) so it
# shows up nicely in the hover tooltip.
code_to_name = {
    f["properties"]["cod_prov"]: f["properties"]["name"]
    for f in spain_geojson["features"]
}
trips["province"] = trips["cod_prov"].map(code_to_name)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Build the choropleth.
#    Plotly matches each row's `locations` value (cod_prov) to the
#    GeoJSON feature identified by `featureidkey`. That IS the join.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
fig = px.choropleth(
    trips,
    geojson=spain_geojson,
    locations="cod_prov",                    # column in our data
    featureidkey="properties.cod_prov",      # matching field in the GeoJSON
    color="total_trips",                     # value that sets the colour
    color_continuous_scale="OrRd",           # light (few) -> dark (many)
    hover_name="province",                   # big label in the tooltip
    hover_data={"cod_prov": False, "total_trips": ":,.0f"},
    labels={"total_trips": "Total trips"},
)

# Zoom to Spain and hide the default world background/graticule.
fig.update_geos(fitbounds="locations", visible=False)

fig.update_layout(
    title_text=(
        f"Trips by province — {SELECTED_DAY} (by trip {PROVINCE_FROM})<br>"
        "<sup>Darker = more trips. A few dark provinces = concentration.</sup>"
    ),
    margin={"r": 0, "t": 60, "l": 0, "b": 0},
)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 5. Show / save
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# In a Jupyter notebook or Colab, this displays the interactive map:
fig.show()

# Save a standalone interactive file you can open in any browser:
fig.write_html("province_choropleth_plotly.html")
print("Saved province_choropleth_plotly.html")

# Quick numeric concentration check
top2 = trips.sort_values("total_trips", ascending=False).head(2)
share = top2["total_trips"].sum() / trips["total_trips"].sum() * 100
print(f"Top 2 provinces = {share:.1f}% of all trips")

6. Read the answer off both charts¶

Now back to the question we started with: on a single day, are trips spread evenly across Spain's provinces, or concentrated in a few? The two charts answer it together — the ranked bar chart puts precise numbers on the busiest provinces, and the choropleth shows where those provinces sit on the map.

The answer is clear: the trips are concentrated, not even. Madrid and Barcelona alone account for close to a third of the day's trips — about 29% between them — and Madrid on its own carries roughly eleven times as many trips as a typical province. On the map, that reads at a glance as two dark provinces standing out against a much paler rest of the country.
